When does your part of the world cross +2 °C?

Annual near-surface air temperature from the NOAA-GFDL GFDL-ESM4 climate model (CMIP6, run r1i1p1f1), averaged from monthly to yearly values, regridded to 5° × 5° cells, and shown as the change from each cell's own 1850–1900 average. Map colours use a 10-year centred running mean to smooth out year-to-year weather; 1850–2014 is the model's historical run, 2015–2100 follows the scenario you pick.

Design rationale

The question is about place and time at once: where does it get warm first, and when? A map alone answers "where" for one year at a time, and a line chart alone answers "when" for one place. So the page links the two. The map is the overview, and clicking any square opens that square's full 1850 to 2100 history beside it, with all three emissions paths and the global average for comparison. Clicking the chart moves the map to that year, so either view can drive the other.

The map uses the Equal Earth projection because it keeps areas true. On Mercator the Arctic, which warms the most, would look several times bigger than it is and exaggerate the story.

Warming is coloured on a red to blue diverging scale centred on zero, so cooling and warming read as opposites and "no change" is white. The scale runs from −3 to +9 °C rather than symmetric limits, because very little of the map ever cools much. I first used −4 to +12 °C so the hottest Arctic squares would not clip, but that made the 2050 map almost white, and most readers will look at the middle of the century. Squares beyond +9 °C clip to the darkest red, and the hover tooltip gives the exact value.

The "Year it crosses" view exists because the year slider makes you watch 240 frames to answer one question. It collapses the whole series into one number per square: the first year after which the square stays above the threshold you pick. That is a single ordered quantity, so it gets a single-hue scale (dark red for early, pale yellow for late) instead of the diverging one, and grey means it does not happen by 2095. The threshold is a slider rather than fixed at 2 °C so you can compare the Paris targets of 1.5 and 2 °C with higher levels.

Data transformations: monthly values are averaged to years, the model's 1° grid is averaged to 5° squares, and each square is measured against its own 1850 to 1900 average. The 5° grid keeps the data file around 3 MB, small enough to load in a browser, while the large patterns stay visible. The map and the crossing years use a 10-year centred running mean, because single years are mostly weather noise and a lucky warm year would otherwise count as a crossing. That mean only has a full window from 1855 to 2095, so the slider and the crossing calculation stop there. An earlier version ran to 2100 and showed some squares crossing in 2100 just because the window was shrinking at the end of the series. The line chart still shows the raw yearly values as faint lines under the smoothed one, so nothing is hidden.

The "Jump to" buttons point at places where the interaction shows something a static global chart cannot: the Arctic passing +2 °C decades before the world does, and a patch of the North Atlantic that cools even on the high emissions path.

Alternatives considered: three small-multiple maps, one per scenario, which made each map too small to read on a laptop; an animation with no linked chart, which shows the pattern but not the numbers for any one place; and only the global average line, which hides the fact that the question has a different answer depending on where you live.

Development process

I did this project alone, because I was not placed on a team. The code and this writeup were written with help from an AI assistant (Claude), which my instructor approved for my situation. I chose the dataset and the question, reviewed the charts, and decided what to keep and change.

The most time went into getting the data. The CMIP6 files on Google Cloud needed specific library versions and a non-standard calendar package before they would open, and that took several attempts. Next was tuning the visualization: the colour scale range, the edge effect of the running mean at 2100, and the line chart's axis, which at first cut off the global average line for squares that warm less than the world. In total the project took about 6 hours.

Data

CMIP6 GFDL-ESM4 (NOAA Geophysical Fluid Dynamics Laboratory), variable tas, monthly, experiments historical, ssp126, ssp245 and ssp585, read from the public CMIP6 archive on Google Cloud. One model and one ensemble member: other models warm more or less (GFDL-ESM4 is on the low side of CMIP6), so treat the numbers as one plausible future, not a forecast. Processing code: scripts/prepare_data.py.
