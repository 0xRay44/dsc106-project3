When does your part of the world cross +2 °C?

Annual near-surface air temperature from the NOAA-GFDL GFDL-ESM4 climate model (CMIP6, run r1i1p1f1), averaged from monthly to yearly values, regridded to 5° × 5° cells, and shown as the change from each cell's own 1850–1900 average. Map colours use a 10-year centred running mean to smooth out year-to-year weather; 1850–2014 is the model's historical run, 2015–2100 follows the scenario you pick.

Design rationale

My question has two parts, where and when, so I used two views. The map shows where it's warming, and the line chart shows when for whatever square you click. Clicking the chart also changes the map's year, so they work together. I used the Equal Earth projection because Mercator makes the Arctic look huge, and the Arctic is already the most extreme part of the map.

For colour I went with red for warmer and blue for cooler, with white at no change. The scale goes from −3 to +9 °C. I tried −4 to +12 first so the hottest Arctic squares wouldn't max out, but then the 2050 map was almost all white, so I shrank it. Anything above +9 just shows as the darkest red, and you can hover for the real number.

The "Year it crosses" mode came from playing with the slider. Watching every year go by to find when a place passes 2 °C was slow, so this mode just colours each square by the year it goes above the threshold and stays there. Earlier is darker red, later is yellow, and grey means it doesn't happen by 2095. You can move the threshold to compare 1.5 °C, 2 °C and higher.

Data steps: monthly values averaged into years, the 1° model grid averaged into 5° squares (this keeps the file at about 3 MB), and each square compared to its own 1850–1900 average. The map uses a 10-year average because single years jump around a lot with the weather. That average needs 5 years on each side, so the slider runs 1855–2095. When it went all the way to 2100, some squares looked like they crossed in 2100, which was just the average running out of years. The line chart still shows every single year as a faint line.

The "Jump to" buttons take you to spots I thought were interesting, like the Arctic passing 2 °C decades before the world does, and part of the North Atlantic that cools even on the high emissions path.

Other ideas: three small maps side by side, one per scenario, but they were too small to read. An animation by itself shows the pattern but not the numbers for one place. A single global average line hides the whole point, which is that the answer depends on where you are.

Development process

I did this one solo since I didn't get put on a team. I used an AI assistant (Claude) for the code and the writeup, which my professor said was fine for my situation. I picked the dataset and the question, looked through the charts, and decided what to keep and change.

It took about 6 hours. The longest part was getting the data to load. The CMIP6 files on Google Cloud needed certain library versions and an extra package for the model's calendar, and that took a few tries. After that it was mostly fixing the visualization: the colour range, the 2100 problem above, and the line chart cutting off the global average for squares that warm less than the world.

Data

CMIP6 GFDL-ESM4 (NOAA Geophysical Fluid Dynamics Laboratory), variable tas, monthly, experiments historical, ssp126, ssp245 and ssp585, read from the public CMIP6 archive on Google Cloud. One model and one ensemble member: other models warm more or less (GFDL-ESM4 is on the low side of CMIP6), so treat the numbers as one plausible future, not a forecast. Processing code: scripts/prepare_data.py.
